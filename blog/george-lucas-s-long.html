George Lucas’s Long: The Obscure Film That Shaped Star Wars—and Modern Cinema

When you think of Star Wars, what comes to mind? The iconic lightsaber duels, the sweeping space battles, the dazzling special effects, or perhaps the way Lucasfilm crafted entire worlds from scratch? While Star Wars itself is a cultural phenomenon, few people realize that its visual language was largely born from a much earlier—and far more experimental—project: George Lucas’s Long.

Released in 1971, Long was Lucas’s first feature-length film, a low-budget, avant-garde sci-fi epic that was never meant for wide release. Yet, its influence on Star Wars is profound. From the way characters move in space to the composition of action sequences, the DNA of Star Wars can be traced back to this forgotten film. Today, we’ll explore how Long became the blueprint for one of the most visually groundbreaking franchises in history—and why its lessons still matter for filmmakers, designers, and storytellers today.

Whether you’re a Star Wars fan, a filmmaker, or simply curious about the creative process behind blockbuster cinema, this deep dive into Long will reveal how a single film—once dismissed as a failure—became the secret weapon behind a cultural revolution.

The Story of Long: A Film That Was Meant to Be Forgotten

In the early 1970s, George Lucas was a rising talent in Hollywood, known for his work on TV shows like The Twilight Zone and Star Trek. But he had bigger ambitions. After watching 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968), Lucas was inspired to create his own sci-fi epic—but with a twist. He wanted a film that wasn’t just about space travel; it was about humanity’s struggle for survival in a dystopian future.

With a budget of just $120,000 (equivalent to around $850,000 today), Lucas shot Long in just 10 weeks on a remote ranch in California. The film was shot in black-and-white (a choice Lucas later regretted) and featured a mix of practical effects, stop-motion animation, and early CGI experiments. Unlike Star Wars, which was meticulously planned, Long was a chaotic process—one that Lucas himself described as a “nightmare”.

The film’s title, Long, was originally meant to be THX 1138, the name of its protagonist. However, due to legal issues (the number was already trademarked), Lucas settled on the shorter, more abstract title. The film was released theatrically in 1971 but performed poorly at the box office. Critics were divided—some praised its bold visuals, while others found it confusing and overly complex.

Despite its lackluster reception, Long was not a complete failure. It won the Grand Prize at the 1971 Cannes Film Festival for its “innovative use of film technology”, and it became a cult favorite among filmmakers. Over time, its influence on Star Wars would become undeniable.

Why Was Long Never a Hit?

  • Poor Marketing: Lucas had no major studio backing, and the film was released in just 12 theaters across the U.S. Most audiences never saw it.
  • Black-and-White Limitation: The lack of color made the film feel dated compared to the vibrant sci-fi films of the time (like Planet of the Apes).
  • Complex Narrative: The film’s non-linear storytelling and dystopian themes were hard to follow for mainstream audiences.
  • Lack of Star Power: Unlike later sci-fi films, Long had no major actors—just a mix of unknowns and Lucas himself in a cameo.

Yet, despite these flaws, Long was a technical masterpiece. Its influence on Star Wars would be subtle but transformative.

How Long Shaped the Visual Language of Star Wars

When Lucas began developing Star Wars in the mid-1970s, he didn’t start from scratch. He refined and expanded on the techniques he had perfected in Long. Here’s how:

1. The Use of Wide Shots and Dynamic Composition

One of the most striking visual choices in Star Wars is its sweeping, cinematic framing. From the opening crawl to the space battles, Lucas used extreme wide shots to immerse the audience in vast, open spaces. This technique was directly inspired by Long.

In Long, Lucas experimented with long takes and unbroken camera movements to create a sense of continuity and flow. For example, the “fight scene” in Long (where the protagonist battles a robotic enforcer) was shot in a single take, a technique later adapted for Star Wars’s lightsaber duels.

Practical Example: The Battle of Yavin in Star Wars: Episode IV uses the same wide-angle framing to make the space battle feel epic and overwhelming. The camera doesn’t cut away—it holds on the action, just like in Long.

2. The Influence of Dystopian Aesthetics

Long was set in a bleak, industrial future where humanity was controlled by a totalitarian regime. This cyberpunk-inspired worldview would later influence Star Wars’s Galactic Empire—the dark, oppressive force that ruled the galaxy.

The gritty, utilitarian design of Long’s sets (think sterile, white corridors and mechanical enforcers) became the blueprint for Star Wars’s Death Star and Imperial Star Destroyers. Even the black armor of stormtroopers echoes the faceless enforcers from Long.

Key Takeaway: If you look closely at Star Wars, you’ll notice that the Empire’s aesthetic is far darker and more industrial than the Rebel Alliance’s organic, warm tones. This contrast was first explored in Long.

3. The Use of Practical Effects Over CGI

Today, CGI dominates blockbuster films, but in the 1970s, Star Wars relied heavily on practical effects. This was a direct result of Lucas’s work on Long, where he had to invent solutions with limited resources.

In Long, Lucas used miniatures, stop-motion, and early CGI experiments to create its futuristic world. When he moved to Star Wars, he perfected these techniques, leading to the iconic X-wing fighters and TIE fighters that became instantly recognizable.

Practical Example: The Death Star trench run in Star Wars was shot using a miniature model of the Death Star, with the camera moving along a track to simulate the pilot’s perspective. This same technique was used in Long for its space chase scenes.

4. The Role of Music in Setting Tone

While Long didn’t have the epic score of Star Wars, its minimalist, electronic soundtrack (composed by Walter Murch) set a precedent for how music could enhance sci-fi immersion.

Lucas later hired John Williams for Star Wars, but the use of electronic and orchestral elements to create a futuristic yet emotional score was first explored in Long.

5. The Concept of “Worldbuilding” Through Visuals

Long was one of the first films to fully realize a fictional universe through visuals alone. The film’s dystopian cityscapes, mechanical enforcers, and sterile environments created a self-contained world that felt real to audiences.

This approach would become essential to Star Wars, where Lucas and his team designed entire planets, spaceships, and cultures before writing a single line of dialogue. The Galactic Empire, the Rebel Alliance, and even the Jedi Order were all visualized first, just as they were in Long.

Modern Parallel: Today, films like Dune and The Mandalorian use the same worldbuilding-through-visuals approach, a technique that can be traced back to Long.

8 Lessons Filmmakers and Designers Can Learn from Long

While Long may not be a household name, its filmmaking techniques are still relevant today. Here are 8 actionable strategies you can apply to your own projects:

  1. Embrace Constraints

    Lucas shot Long with a tiny budget and limited resources. Instead of seeing limitations as obstacles, he used them creatively. Today, filmmakers and designers can work within constraints to force innovation. For example, if you don’t have access to expensive CGI, use practical effects, stop-motion, or even hand-drawn animation to achieve the same visual impact.

  2. Prioritize Visual Storytelling

    Long told its story primarily through visuals, not dialogue. This is a powerful lesson for modern filmmakers. Instead of relying on heavy exposition, use composition, lighting, and movement to convey emotion and plot. For example, a close-up of a character’s trembling hands can say more than a page of dialogue.

  3. Experiment with Unconventional Framing

    Lucas used wide shots, extreme angles, and long takes in Long to create a cinematic experience. Today, filmmakers can play with framing to enhance tension or immersion. For example, shooting a chase scene from a low angle makes the action feel more intense.
