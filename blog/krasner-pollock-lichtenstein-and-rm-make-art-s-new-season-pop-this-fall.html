Krasner & Pollock, Lichtenstein and RM: How Art’s New Season Pop This Fall

Fall is the season when the art world shifts into high gear. Galleries reopen after summer lulls, auction houses prepare for their biggest sales of the year, and artists—both established and emerging—launch new projects that challenge and captivate audiences. This year, the fall art season is particularly vibrant, with a striking convergence of legacy and innovation. Iconic figures like Lee Krasner and Jackson Pollock, whose work defined mid-century abstract expressionism, are being reimagined through contemporary lenses. Meanwhile, Roy Lichtenstein, the godfather of pop art, continues to inspire new generations of artists, while RM—the globally renowned K-pop star and artist—is making waves with his bold, boundary-pushing creations.

This blend of historical reverence and modern experimentation isn’t just a fleeting trend; it reflects a broader cultural moment where art consumers crave depth, nostalgia, and fresh perspectives simultaneously. Whether you're an art collector, a student of creative processes, or simply someone who appreciates the visual arts, understanding these trends can offer valuable insights into how art evolves—and why it continues to resonate across generations.

In this deep dive, we’ll explore how Krasner, Pollock, Lichtenstein, and RM are shaping the fall art season, the techniques and strategies behind their influence, and how you can engage with these movements—whether through collecting, creating, or simply appreciating art in your daily life.

The Legacy of Krasner and Pollock: Abstract Expressionism Reimagined

Lee Krasner and Jackson Pollock are two of the most celebrated figures of abstract expressionism, a movement that emerged in New York in the 1940s and 1950s. Their work, characterized by bold brushstrokes, dynamic compositions, and a deep emotional intensity, continues to inspire artists today. Yet, their legacy isn’t static; it’s being reinterpreted through contemporary eyes, blending historical techniques with modern sensibilities.

Who Were Krasner and Pollock?

Jackson Pollock (1912–1956) is perhaps the most famous of the abstract expressionists, known for his drip paintings, where he poured and splashed paint onto canvas laid on the floor. His work, like "No. 5, 1948", is celebrated for its raw energy and spontaneous creation. Lee Krasner (1908–1984), Pollock’s wife and collaborator, was equally groundbreaking. Her work often featured layered, textured surfaces and a more controlled yet equally expressive approach. Together, they embodied the movement’s duality: Pollock’s chaotic freedom and Krasner’s structured intensity.

Why Their Work Still Matters Today

The enduring appeal of Krasner and Pollock lies in their ability to capture the human experience—emotion, movement, and the subconscious—without relying on representational imagery. In an era dominated by digital art and algorithmic aesthetics, their work offers a tactile, physical connection to creativity. Galleries and museums are capitalizing on this by hosting retrospectives, exhibitions, and even artist residencies that encourage modern creators to engage with their techniques.

For example, the Whitney Museum of American Art recently hosted an exhibition titled "Lee Krasner: Living Color", which highlighted her vibrant, often overlooked use of color. Meanwhile, contemporary artists like Julie Mehretu and Mark Bradford have cited Krasner and Pollock as influences in their layered, abstract works.

How Contemporary Artists Are Reinterpreting Their Work

Today’s artists are taking cues from Krasner and Pollock in three key ways:

  1. Material Experimentation: Pollock’s drip technique has been adapted into 3D installations, where artists use paint, resin, and even liquid metal to create dynamic, textured surfaces. For instance, Alex Israel blends Pollock’s fluidity with digital manipulation, creating paintings that feel both ancient and futuristic.
  2. Layering and Collage: Krasner’s layered approach is evident in the work of Kara Walker, who uses cut-paper silhouettes to build complex narratives. Similarly, Cindy Sherman employs layered photography to explore identity and representation.
  3. Emotional Directness: Both Krasner and Pollock prioritized raw emotion over technical perfection. Artists like Wangechi Mutu carry this ethos forward, using bold, expressive strokes to address themes of race, gender, and power.

Practical Takeaways for Artists and Collectors

If you're an artist looking to engage with abstract expressionism, consider these actionable strategies:

  • Embrace Imperfection: Pollock’s drip paintings weren’t meticulously planned; they emerged from spontaneous action. Try working on the floor with large canvases to experience the same sense of freedom.
  • Experiment with Materials: Use unconventional tools like spray paint, sand, or even food coloring to create textured surfaces, as Krasner did with her combings technique.
  • Study the Process: Watch documentaries like "Jackson Pollock: An American Master" to understand their creative rituals. Krasner, for example, often worked in series, building on previous ideas.
  • Blend Tradition with Modernity: Incorporate digital tools—like procreate brushes that mimic Pollock’s drips—while maintaining a handmade, organic feel.

For collectors, Krasner and Pollock’s work remains a safe yet high-value investment. Auction houses like Christie’s and Sotheby’s continue to see strong demand for their pieces, with records being broken regularly. For example, Pollock’s "No. 5, 1948" sold for $140 million in 2006, and Krasner’s "The Seasons" series has also seen significant appreciation.

Roy Lichtenstein: The Pop Art Pioneer Who Still Rules the Scene

While Krasner and Pollock dominated the abstract world, Roy Lichtenstein (1923–1997) was revolutionizing art with his pop art approach. His large-scale, comic-book-inspired paintings—like "Whaam!" (1963)—challenged the boundaries between high art and mass culture. Decades later, Lichtenstein’s influence is more pronounced than ever, with artists and designers continuing to draw from his bold colors, geometric precision, and playful irony.

The Pop Art Revolution and Its Lasting Impact

Lichtenstein emerged in the 1960s as part of the pop art movement, which sought to democratize art by incorporating imagery from advertising, comics, and everyday life. His work was a direct response to the commercialization of culture, using Benzedrine pens, halftone dots, and comic strips to create paintings that felt both accessible and provocative.

Today, Lichtenstein’s legacy is evident in:

  • Graphic Design: Brands like Nike and Adidas use Lichtenstein-inspired aesthetics in their packaging and advertisements.
  • Street Art: Artists like Banksy and Shepard Fairey incorporate Lichtenstein’s bold outlines and color blocking into their murals.
  • Digital Art: NFT projects often reference Lichtenstein’s style, using pixelation and comic-book motifs to create digital collectibles.

How Contemporary Artists Are Channeling Lichtenstein

Lichtenstein’s techniques are being adapted in three distinct ways:

  1. Comic Book Aesthetics: Artists like Zdzisław Beksiński and Takashi Murakami blend Lichtenstein’s bold lines with surreal, often dystopian imagery. Murakami, in particular, has created entire superflat movements inspired by pop art’s flat, two-dimensional quality.
  2. Geometric Abstraction: Lichtenstein’s use of halftone dots has influenced Op Art and minimalist designers. Artists like Bruno Munari use similar techniques to create optical illusions.
  3. Irony and Satire: Lichtenstein often parodied consumer culture. Today, artists like Ai Weiwei use pop art techniques to critique modern society, blending Lichtenstein’s humor with sharp social commentary.

Common Mistakes to Avoid When Engaging with Pop Art

While Lichtenstein’s style is widely admired, there are pitfalls to avoid if you're trying to incorporate his techniques into your own work:

  • Over-Reliance on Clichés: Lichtenstein’s success came from his subversion of clichés, not their repetition. Avoid simply copying comic book panels without adding a critical or creative twist.
  • Ignoring the Context: Pop art was a cultural critique. Without understanding the historical and social context, your work may feel hollow or superficial.
  • Neglecting the Technical Precision: Lichtenstein’s dots and lines were meticulously planned. Rushing the process can lead to a lack of cohesion in your piece.

Investing in Pop Art: What to Look For

If you're interested in collecting pop art, focus on:

  • Early Works: Lichtenstein’s paintings from the 1960s, like "Drowning Girl", often command higher prices due to their historical significance.
  • Collaborations: Lichtenstein worked with designers and other artists, creating limited-edition prints and sculptures that can be more affordable entry points.
  • Contemporary Pop Art: Artists like Jeff Koons and Keith Haring continue the tradition, offering modern interpretations with strong market appeal.

RM: The K-Pop Star Who’s Redefining Art in 2024

While Krasner, Pollock, and Lichtenstein represent the canonical figures of modern art, RM—the lead vocalist of BTS—is one of the most unexpected yet influential artists of the 21st century. A global superstar with a deep appreciation for visual arts, RM has transitioned from music to painting with remarkable skill, blending abstract expressionism, pop culture, and personal symbolism in his work.

From K-Pop to Canvas: RM’s Artistic Journey

RM’s foray into art began as a passion project, but it quickly gained attention for its bold, emotive quality. His paintings often feature:

  • Vibrant Colors: Inspired by the energy of K-pop performances, RM’s work is characterized by high-contrast hues and dynamic compositions.
  • Abstract Symbolism: Many of his pieces incorporate personal motifs, such as the number 7 (a reference to his birth month) and geometric shapes that evoke movement.
  • Collaborative Elements: RM has worked with other artists and even AI tools to explore new creative directions, blending traditional techniques with digital innovation.

How RM’s Art Connects with Fall Trends

RM’s work resonates with the fall art season in three key ways:

  1. Global Appeal: As a cultural icon, RM’s art bridges East and West, attracting collectors from diverse backgrounds. This aligns with the fall season’s emphasis on international exhibitions and cross-cultural collaborations.
  2. Youth and Innovation: RM’s audience is predominantly young, and his art reflects a digital-native sensibility. This makes his work particularly relevant as galleries and museums seek to engage younger audiences.
  3. Emotional Depth: Like Krasner and Pollock, RM’s work is deeply personal. His paintings often explore themes of identity, resilience, and self-expression, themes that resonate with contemporary viewers.
