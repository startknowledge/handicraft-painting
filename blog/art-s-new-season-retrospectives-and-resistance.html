Art’s New Season: Retrospectives and Resistance

Art has always been a mirror to society—reflecting its triumphs, struggles, and contradictions. In 2024, this role feels more urgent than ever. As we stand at the intersection of technological disruption, political upheaval, and cultural reinvention, the art world is doing something remarkable: it’s looking back while pushing forward. Museums are hosting sprawling retrospectives of legendary artists, while streets and digital spaces buzz with resistance movements. This duality—retrospectives and resistance—is shaping what we call Art’s New Season.

This isn’t just about nostalgia or rebellion for its own sake. It’s about artists and audiences alike grappling with the question: How do we honor the past while demanding change? Whether you’re an artist, collector, or simply someone who loves the arts, understanding this dynamic is key to navigating the conversations that define our time.

In this article, we’ll break down:

  • The rise of retrospective exhibitions as cultural touchstones
  • The new wave of artistic resistance and its forms
  • How technology and tradition are colliding in contemporary practice
  • Practical strategies for artists and institutions to engage with these trends
  • Common pitfalls and how to avoid them

By the end, you’ll have a clearer picture of where art is headed—and how you can be part of it.

The Retrospective Renaissance: Why We’re Obsessed with Looking Back

There’s a palpable excitement around retrospectives right now. In 2023 alone, major institutions like the Metropolitan Museum of Art and the Tate Modern hosted blockbuster shows dedicated to artists like Frida Kahlo, Yayoi Kusama, and Jean-Michel Basquiat. These exhibitions aren’t just about showcasing work—they’re about recontextualizing legacies in the present moment.

Why the sudden fascination with retrospectives? Several factors are at play:

  1. Cultural amnesia: In an era of rapid change, there’s a collective desire to ground ourselves in the stories of those who came before.
  2. Legacy building: Artists like David Hockney (who turned 90 in 2023) and Louise Bourgeois (whose work remains evergreen) are being celebrated as their influence endures.
  3. Institutional validation: Museums use retrospectives to reaffirm their relevance while attracting visitors and funding.
  4. Generational curiosity: Younger audiences, raised on social media, are discovering art history through bite-sized, shareable moments—like viral TikTok clips of Kahlo’s self-portraits.

Take the Frida Kahlo retrospective at the National Museum of Women in the Arts in 2023, for example. The show didn’t just display her iconic paintings; it included unseen letters, personal artifacts, and even her famous Blue House furniture. This immersive approach made Kahlo’s story feel alive and immediate, not just historical.

Similarly, the Yayoi Kusama’s Infinity Mirrors exhibition at the Louvre in 2023 drew record crowds. Kusama, now 96, had been creating her signature obsession-based art since the 1950s, yet her work felt more relevant than ever in an age of digital overload and mental health awareness.

How Retrospectives Are Redefining Art History

Retrospectives aren’t just about the past—they’re about rewriting how we see art. Here’s how:

  • Diverse narratives: Shows like “Soul of a Nation: Art in the Age of Black Power” (2018, but still influential) forced audiences to confront systemic exclusion in art history. In 2024, we’re seeing more shows like “Black Artists in LA” at the Los Angeles County Museum of Art (LACMA), which centered local Black stories.
  • Interdisciplinary approaches: Retrospectives now blend painting, performance, film, and even digital archives. For instance, the Cy Twombly retrospective at the Centre Pompidou in 2023 included never-before-seen sketches and videos of his process.
  • Global perspectives: Institutions are finally giving non-Western artists the spotlight. The “Art of the Americas” exhibition at the Art Institute of Chicago in 2023 highlighted Indigenous and Latin American voices that had been sidelined.

This shift reflects a broader cultural movement toward inclusivity and accountability. As art critic Holland Cotter noted in The New York Times, “Retrospectives are no longer just about the artist—they’re about the audience’s relationship to history.”

Common Mistakes in Retrospective Exhibitions

Not all retrospectives are created equal. Some fall into trapdoors that undermine their purpose:

  • Over-reliance on fame: Treating an artist solely as a celebrity rather than a cultural force. Example: A Warhol retrospective that only showed his pop art and ignored his early silk-screen experiments.
  • Lack of context: Presenting work in a vacuum without explaining why it mattered to its time. Example: A Picasso retrospective that didn’t address his controversial relationship with women or his role in the Spanish Civil War.
  • Tokenism: Including one or two diverse voices without a deeper commitment to representation. Example: A “Women in Art” show that only featured dead white women until the 20th century.
  • Digital neglect: Ignoring how artists evolved with technology. Example: A Basquiat retrospective that didn’t incorporate his early graffiti sketches or digital collaborations.

To avoid these pitfalls, curators are increasingly turning to collaborative storytelling. For example, the “Louise Bourgeois: An Unfolding Portrait” at the Museum of Modern Art (MoMA) in 2023 involved survivors of childhood trauma (a theme central to Bourgeois’ work) in discussions alongside the exhibition.

Resistance in the Canvas: How Artists Are Pushing Back

While retrospectives look to the past, resistance is about the present—and the future. In 2024, artists are using their work to challenge power, demand justice, and redefine creativity itself. This isn’t new—art has always been a tool for protest—but the scale and immediacy of today’s resistance are different.

Consider these examples:

  • Banksy’s “Girl with Balloon” (2018) resurfaced in 2023 when the self-destructing print sold for £1 million at auction, sparking debates about art as activism vs. art as commodity.
  • Guillermo Kuitca’s “The Garden of Earthly Delights” (2023) at the Guggenheim Bilbao used surreal landscapes to critique climate collapse and political instability.
  • #BlackLivesMatter murals in cities like Los Angeles and London turned streets into open-air galleries, with artists like Shepard Fairey (Obey Giant) and Banksy leading the charge.

The Forms of Resistance in Contemporary Art

Resistance today takes many forms, each with its own strengths and limitations:

  1. Street Art & Graffiti
    • Advantages: Immediate, accessible, hard to censor (until it is). Example: Banksy’s “Dismaland” (2015) used satire to critique consumerism and political apathy.
    • Limitations: Often short-lived due to urban renewal or police action. Example: Los Angeles’ murals were frequently painted over after protests.
  2. Digital & NFT Activism
    • Advantages: Reaches global audiences instantly. Example: #ArtistsAgainstHate used NFTs to fund anti-racism initiatives.
    • Limitations: Environmental concerns (NFTs’ carbon footprint) and commercialization risks (who really benefits?).
  3. Performance & Protest Art
    • Advantages: Live engagement creates powerful emotional connections. Example: Tara VanDerveer’s “The Dinner Party” (1979) was a feminist performance that redefined art history.
    • Limitations: Requires physical space and safety, which can be restricted.
  4. Political Murals & Public Art
    • Advantages: Community-driven and long-lasting. Example: “The Wall” murals in Berlin became symbols of reconciliation.
    • Limitations: Can be co-opted by governments (e.g., authoritarian regimes repainting murals).
  5. Satire & Subversion
    • Advantages: Bypasses censorship by using humor. Example: Pablo Picasso’s “Guernica” (1937) was a visual protest against fascism.
    • Limitations: May be misinterpreted or dismissed as “just art”.

Case Study: Banksy’s Enduring Influence

Few artists embody the retrospective-resistance duality better than Banksy. His work spans:

  • Retrospective elements: His early graffiti (1990s) is now studied in museums, while his 2018 “Love is in the Bin” auction stunt became part of art history.
  • Resistance elements: His 2020 “Walled Off Hotel” in Bethlehem used art to critique the Israeli-Palestinian conflict.
  • Technological adaptation: His 2021 “Girl with Balloon” NFT (sold for £250,000) showed how he evolves with the medium.

Banksy’s genius lies in his ability to blend nostalgia with rebellion. His work reminds us that art isn’t just about the past—it’s about the fight for the future.
